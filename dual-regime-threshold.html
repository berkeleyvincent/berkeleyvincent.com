Naval Nuclear Regulatory Architecture

Navigating the Dual-Regime Threshold: Sovereign Licensing, Safety Architecture, and Waste Stewardship Under the AUKUS Submarine Delivery and Civil Moratorium Frameworks

Executive Abstract: Australia's strategic commitment under the AUKUS partnership to acquire nuclear-powered attack submarines (SSNs) has mandated the creation of the nation's first sovereign naval nuclear enterprise. To govern these defense activities, the Commonwealth established the Australian Nuclear-Powered Submarine Safety Regulator (APSSR) outside the civil safety framework directed by the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency (ARPANSA). Concurrently, civil nuclear energy remains legally prohibited under Section 140A of the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 (EPBC Act) and Section 10 of the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Act 1998 (ARPANS Act). This institutional bifurcation introduces structural frictions across safety-case methodologies, personnel certification, supply-chain quality assurance, and radioactive waste management. This paper investigates the statutory, engineering, and lifecycle interfaces between the defense naval framework and the civil regulatory baseline. We propose a statutory "Dual-Regime Safety Envelope" (DRSE) anchored by an inter-agency concordat and a shared National Nuclear Technical Support Organization (NN-TSO), culminating in a phased 2026–2035 technical roadmap.

1. Introduction and Statutory Context

The trilateral AUKUS agreement represents the most technologically complex industrial endeavor in Australian history. Delivering nuclear-powered attack submarines requires navigating unprecedented regulatory terrain: introducing military naval reactors into an operational environment historically constrained by a federal statutory ban on commercial nuclear installations.

1.1 The Legislative Anatomy of the Moratorium

Federal prohibition on civil nuclear energy is codified primarily in two statutes:

  • EPBC Act 1999 (Cth) Section 140A: Explicitly prohibits the Minister from approving an action consisting of or involving the construction or operation of a nuclear fuel fabrication plant, nuclear power station, enrichment facility, or reprocessing facility.
  • ARPANS Act 1998 (Cth) Section 10: Explicitly bars the CEO of ARPANSA from issuing a facility or source license for the purpose of a nuclear fuel fabrication plant, nuclear power station, enrichment plant, or reprocessing plant.

These provisions were enacted to ring-fence research reactor operations at Lucas Heights (the legacy HIFAR and subsequent OPAL reactor) while preventing the emergence of a commercial nuclear power sector.

1.2 Establishment of the APSSR

To accommodate Pillar I of AUKUS, the Commonwealth enacted the Australian Nuclear-Powered Submarine Safety Regulator Act 2023. The Act established the APSSR as an independent statutory agency within the Defence portfolio, reporting directly to the Minister for Defence. The APSSR is tasked exclusively with regulating the safety and radiological protection aspects of naval nuclear-powered submarines and their dedicated support infrastructure.

1.3 Problem Statement: The Risks of Institutional Bifurcation

The parallel operation of APSSR and ARPANSA produces a dual-regime paradigm. While defense security necessitates strict control over classified technology, bifurcating national nuclear oversight across two regulators risks duplicating scarce sovereign technical resources, fracturing the domestic supply chain, and establishing conflicting safety philosophies across state and federal jurisdictions.

2. International Comparative Architectures

2.1 The United Kingdom Model: DNSR and ONR Interface

In the United Kingdom, naval nuclear activities are regulated by the Defence Nuclear Safety Regulator (DNSR), while civil nuclear facilities are governed by the Office for Nuclear Regulation (ONR):

  • Dual-Regulated Sites: Industrial shipyards (such as BAE Systems at Barrow-in-Furness) and naval bases (such as HMNB Devonport) operate under joint DNSR/ONR oversight via formal Letters of Understanding.
  • Technical Consistency: The DNSR aligns its regulatory standards directly with the ONR's Safety Assessment Principles (SAPs), ensuring engineering equivalency between civil commercial designs and naval propulsion systems.

2.2 The United States Model: Naval Reactors (08 Program) and the NRC

The US model features a deliberate statutory separation established under the Atomic Energy Act of 1954:

  • Naval Reactors (Director, Naval Nuclear Propulsion / NAVSEA 08 / NNSA): Retains absolute sovereign authority over all aspects of naval reactor design, safety, personnel qualification, and lifecycle management under Section 91(b).
  • Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC): Exercises zero oversight over naval propulsion systems, focusing strictly on civil commercial reactors (10 CFR Parts 50 and 52) and materials licensing.
  • Structural Implications: While effective in the US due to the immense scale of its defense industrial complex, attempting to replicate this absolute separation in Australia risks isolating a nascent engineering workforce without the critical mass of domestic personnel.

3. Core Engineering Interfaces and Technical Friction Points

3.1 Safety Case Methodologies: Deterministic vs. Probabilistic Paradigms

  • Naval Safety Envelope: Naval nuclear propulsion systems are optimized for combat survivability, spatial compactness, and extreme mechanical shock. Naval safety cases rely primarily on deterministic engineering boundaries, single-failure criteria, structural over-design, and passive decay heat dissipation without off-vessel logistical dependence.
  • Civil Safety Envelope: Civil nuclear plants, governed internationally by IAEA Safety Standards (SSR-2/1), rely predominantly on Level 1, 2, and 3 Probabilistic Safety Assessments (PSA). Regulatory acceptance hinges on quantitative risk metrics: Core Damage Frequency ($$\text{CDF} < 10^{-5}$$ per reactor year) and Large Early Release Frequency ($$\text{LERF} < 10^{-6}$$ per reactor year).
  • Interface Friction: Naval safety data cannot easily integrate civil PSA justifications without declassifying proprietary naval transient and burnup codes. Conversely, civil regulators cannot accept unverified deterministic claims for facilities situated near urban population centers.

3.2 Human Capital Architecture: SQEP Matrix and License Portability

  • Operator Qualification: Naval Reactor Operator (NRO) certifications are embedded in naval military hierarchies, while civil reactor operators (RO/SRO) hold independent statutory licenses.
  • Radiological Protection: Harmonization between military operational dosimetry and the civil Australian National Radiation Dose Register (ANRDR) is required to ensure seamless career mobility between naval shipyards and civil research/industrial institutions.
  • Engineering Signatory Authority: Formal accreditation bridges must be codified between Naval Technical Regulatory Authority (TRA) signatory delegations and the Engineers Australia Chartered Professional Engineer (CPEng Nuclear) register.

3.3 Manufacturing Codes, Standards, and Supply-Chain Quality Assurance

  • Military Naval Standards: NAVSEA 250-1500-1 (welding and NDT), Submarine Safety (SUBSAFE) certification, and DEF STAN 02-770.
  • Civil Nuclear Codes: ASME Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code Section III (Rules for Construction of Nuclear Facility Components) and ISO 19443 (Nuclear Quality Management).
  • Economic Impact: A dual-audit burden forces domestic Tier-2/3 manufacturers to choose between exclusive defense work or civil export markets. Establishing formal standards equivalencies is vital to sovereign industrial viability.

4. Radioactive Waste and Complete Fuel Cycle Governance

4.1 The Complete Fuel Cycle Paradox

  • Fuel Characteristics: US Virginia-class and UK SSN-AUKUS submarines utilize highly enriched uranium (HEU, nominal >93% \(^{235}\text{U}\)) life-of-ship reactor cores designed for 30+ year operation without refueling.
  • Criticality Safety: Spent naval cores retain significant unburnt fissile material at end-of-life. Canister designs must maintain an effective multiplication factor of: $$k_{\text{eff}} + 2\sigma \le 0.95$$ under all credible post-closure scenarios, including full water flooding.
  • Decay Heat and Packaging: Naval cores avoid civil open spent fuel pools; decommissioned reactor compartments (RCPs) or defueled core modules require heavy-walled dry-storage containment with passive natural convection.

4.2 Regulatory Ownership and Transport Boundaries

The APSSR Act governs radioactive waste generated from defense naval propulsion, while the ARPANS Act and State radiation acts govern civil radioactive materials and common-carrier transit corridors. Transporting intermediate-level waste (ILW) and high-level waste (HLW) across state borders demands an integrated transport licensing mechanism adhering to IAEA SSR-6 (Rev. 1) to eliminate inter-jurisdictional impasses.

4.3 Deep Geological Repository (DGR) Engineering and IAEA Safeguards

  • Cratonic Stability: The crystalline basement formations of the Gawler and Yilgarn cratons provide world-class geological isolation.
  • Engineered Barrier Thresholds: Multi-barrier repository designs must strictly limit bentonite clay buffer temperatures to < 100°C to prevent illitization and loss of swelling capacity. This mandates a 40- to 60-year surface dry-storage cooling period for high-heat naval components before deep geological emplacement.
  • IAEA Article 14 Verification: Under Australia's Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement (INFCIRC/217), safeguards re-attach when spent naval fuel is permanently declared as waste. Facilities must feature Safeguards-by-Design (SBD) and information barriers to verify fissile material presence without disclosing classified core geometries.

5.1 The Statutory Concordat and Information Air-Gap

  • Demarcation: APSSR maintains exclusive jurisdiction over naval vessels, naval reactor compartments, and sovereign defense maintenance precincts. ARPANSA maintains jurisdiction over civil off-site emergency planning zones, regional environmental surveillance, and civil transport corridors.
  • Approved Entity Gateway: ARPANSA accepts APSSR-certified bounding envelope metrics (maximum credible release source terms and thermal discharge curves) without requiring line-by-line inspection of proprietary primary-plant technical specifications.

5.2 National Nuclear Technical Support Organization (NN-TSO)

  • Shared Modeling Bench: Provides sovereign verification of computational fluid dynamics, reactor physics, and seismic fracture mechanics.
  • Cleared Engineering Cell: Employs TSPV-cleared personnel to evaluate classified naval datasets alongside unclassified civilian IAEA safety analyses.

5.3 Scalability to Civil Small Modular Reactors (SMRs)

The infrastructure, human capital, and regulatory standards developed for naval propulsion provide the direct foundation for future civil SMRs: establishing precedent for integral Pressurized Water Reactor (iPWR) passive decay heat licensing, shipyard modular fabrication techniques, and Emergency Planning Zone (EPZ) boundary minimization.

6. Strategic Synthesis and Technical Roadmap (2026–2035)

Phase I: Bilateral Foundation and Regulatory Seam Harmonization (2026–2028)

  • Enact APSSR-ARPANSA statutory concordat defining spatial and jurisdictional seams.
  • Establish the National Nuclear Standards Committee (NNSC) under Standards Australia to formalize ASME Section III / SUBSAFE / ISO 19443 equivalencies.
  • License HMAS Stirling radiological support and operational berthing perimeters.
  • Integrate military operational dosimetry with the Australian National Radiation Dose Register.

Phase II: Industrial Scaling and Sovereign SQEP Pipeline (2029–2031)

  • Fully commission the National Nuclear Technical Support Organization (NN-TSO) at ANSTO.
  • Codify the Engineers Australia nuclear competency register for military-to-civilian license portability.
  • Issue site and environmental licenses for the Osborne SSN-AUKUS shipyard.
  • Finalize technical criteria and site selection for sovereign interim dry-storage modules.

Phase III: Full Operational Capability and Dual-Regime Maturity (2032–2035)

  • License and accept the first sovereign Australian-flagged Virginia-class submarine under APSSR authority.
  • Deploy the DRSE to establish Generic Design Assessment (GDA) pre-licensing pathways for civil SMRs should federal moratorium provisions be repealed.
  • Execute full IAEA Article 14 safeguards verification protocols for decommissioned naval components.
  • Complete exploratory characterization boreholes for the permanent cratonic deep geological repository.
